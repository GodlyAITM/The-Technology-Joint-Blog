If you work from home, even part-time, your living room has quietly turned into an office network with no IT department watching over it. That's not a scare tactic — it's just where remote and hybrid work landed. The router streaming your evening's shows is the same one carrying client files, invoices, and login sessions.

Securing that setup doesn't take enterprise tools or an IT background, though. It takes a handful of decisions made once, plus a couple of habits kept up over time. This guide walks through both, roughly in the order they matter most.

## Why home networks are a bigger target than people assume

Attackers rarely need to break into a company's servers when an employee's home network will do. One unpatched router, one reused password, one convincing phishing email — that's often enough to reach the same client data a company firewall would normally sit in front of.

Solo remote workers and small offices are, if anything, *more* attractive targets than they'd guess. They tend to hold valuable data — client records, financial details, business accounts — without the security budget a bigger company would have behind it. Small doesn't mean overlooked. Often it means easier.

## Five layers, roughly in order of importance

Rather than a flat checklist, it helps to think in layers. Each protects against a different kind of failure.

### 1. Your network (the router)

The router is the front door. If it's misconfigured or running old firmware, everything behind it — laptops, phones, smart devices — inherits that weakness.

At minimum:

- Change the router's default admin username and password. Not the Wi-Fi password — the admin login, which controls the router's own settings.
- Keep firmware updated. Most modern routers can do this automatically once you turn the option on.
- Use **WPA3-Personal** if every device on your network supports it, or **WPA2/WPA3 Transition Mode** (sometimes labeled "mixed mode") if you've got a mix of newer and older hardware.
- Turn off remote management unless you specifically need to access your router's settings from outside your home.

### 2. Your devices

Every laptop, phone, and tablet connected to your work is a potential entry point.

- Keep operating systems and apps updated — most breaches exploit a vulnerability the manufacturer already patched.
- Confirm full-disk encryption is actually on. It's automatic on iPhones and iPads, and increasingly automatic on new Windows and Mac machines — but not guaranteed on every device or Windows edition, so it's worth a quick check rather than an assumption.
- Lock every device with a PIN, password, or biometric. No exceptions, including the tablet that "never leaves the house."
- Run antivirus software on Windows machines in particular. *(See our [antivirus software comparison] for current picks.)*

### 3. Your accounts

This is where most real breaches actually happen — not sophisticated hacking, but weak or reused passwords and accounts with no second layer of protection.

- Use a password manager instead of memorized or reused passwords.
- Turn on two-factor authentication for email, banking, and anything tied to client work — an authenticator app rather than a text message where you have the choice.
- Protect your email account especially closely. It's usually the recovery method for everything else you own, which makes it the single highest-value target you have.

*(The full reasoning behind this — including what changed in the official password guidelines this year — is in [Password Security in 2026: What Actually Matters].)*

### 4. Your behavior

Tools only work if the habits around them hold up too. Most successful attacks on remote workers rely on a moment of inattention, not a technical flaw.

- Treat unexpected attachments and links with suspicion, even from senders who look familiar.
- Confirm unusual payment or data requests through a second channel — a phone call, not a reply to the same email thread the request came in on.
- Skip sensitive work on public Wi-Fi unless you're on a VPN.

### 5. Your recovery plan

Assume something will eventually go wrong, and have an answer ready before it does.

- Know how to change your passwords and cut off device access quickly if an account is compromised.
- Keep backups of important files somewhere separate from your primary device.
- Know who to call — your bank, your clients, your email provider — the moment something looks wrong.

## A realistic starting point, not a perfect one

You don't need all five layers finished this weekend. If you do nothing else this month, do these three, in this order:

1. Set a unique password for your email account and turn on two-factor authentication.
1. Change your router's default admin credentials.
1. Start using a password manager for everything else.

Everything past that still matters — it's just less likely to be the thing that actually gets you compromised.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I really need all of this as one person working from home, not a whole office?**\
If anything, more so. A solo remote worker usually has no backup systems or IT support to catch a mistake, which makes prevention carry more weight, not less.

**Is a VPN necessary at home, or just for public Wi-Fi?**\
A VPN matters most on networks you don't control — a café, an airport, a co-working space. At home, a properly secured router does more of the heavy lifting than a VPN would.

**What's the single most common mistake remote workers make?**\
Reusing the same password across accounts. One password leaked from a site that has nothing to do with your work becomes a master key into everything else, the moment it's reused.
